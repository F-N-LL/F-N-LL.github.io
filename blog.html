A map is not
the territory.

What old navigators can teach us about building systems in the age of AI.

A diagram gives us the reassuring shape of a system: boxes, arrows, and a destination. Production introduces the weather. A dependency slows down. A dataset arrives incomplete. A user asks a question nobody put on the chart.

The useful response is to improve our instruments. Logs tell us where we have been. Metrics give us bearings. Traces show the path of a single request through unfamiliar waters. Together, they let us compare the system we imagined with the one we actually built.

New waters, familiar discipline

AI adds another kind of uncertainty. A convincing answer can still be wrong, and a successful demonstration can hide a fragile workflow. Before building around a model, I want to understand what good means, how we will measure it, and what should happen when it fails.

A small, representative evaluation set is a useful first chart. Human review, explicit boundaries, and a recoverable fallback give the team room to explore without confusing movement with progress.

Leave room to redraw the coast

The best architecture preserves our ability to learn. Keep the parts we understand simple. Make uncertain decisions reversible. Record assumptions so that the next engineer can see where the coastline is still a guess.

We build a map to help us sail. We keep looking out of the window to know where we are.
